Fable 5.1 Ships Cheaper, Sharper, Watched Closer

Anthropic · September 1, 2026 · 1,359 pts on HN · 1,311 comments

Anthropic's pitch for Fable 5.1 and its access-gated twin Mythos 5.1 is less about a single benchmark leap than about economics: cache reads now cost 75% less ($0.25/M tokens), which nets out to roughly 25% cheaper typical usage and up to 45% off for heavy agentic workloads. The capability story is real too — Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 more than doubled from Fable 5's 24.7% to 52.6%, and Millennium's quants say it found the root cause of a crash that had eluded their engineers, and every other model, for years.

Mythos 5.1 is the same weights with looser safety rails, sold through vetted cyber and life-sciences access programs rather than to the public. Anthropic is leaning hard into the scientific angle: a new Venus elevation map built from 30-year-old Magellan radar data, protein binders hitting nearly 50% hit rates against a 10-15% industry norm, and GPU kernel rewrites that cut genomics inference costs by up to 60%.

HN's harder edge landed on the model's prose, not its scores — a long thread on "comment slop," with engineers complaining Claude-written code comments have become bloated notes-to-self. The bigger tension nobody fully resolved: new anti-distillation measures that quietly break a documented technique for extracting Claude's reasoning traces, alongside EU-mandated watermarking, arrive framed as safety even as they double as moat-building against smaller competitors copying the model's homework.

"It found the cause of a crash that none of its engineers — or any other model — had explained in years."

Dan Luu Grades the Loudest AI Skeptic's Homework

danluu.com · September 2, 2026 · 835 pts on HN · 972 comments

Dan Luu, who discloses he holds no meaningful stake in AI either way, went back through two years of Ed Zitron's predictions and found the track record thin. The anchor example: a November 2024 talk calling Meta "a dying product, and kind of a dying company." Luu lines that up against Meta's actual revenue and GAAP operating income over the following year, both of which kept climbing, and argues Zitron's method — big conclusions from shaky proxies like third-party traffic estimates rather than the companies' own filings — is the real problem, not any single bad call.

The piece is less a hit job than an audit of reasoning quality, and Luu is explicit that he's previously been just as harsh on techno-optimist futurists like Kurzweil. That evenhandedness is what pushed the thread past 970 comments: HN's AI skeptics didn't abandon the underlying bear case so much as relitigate it on cleaner terms.

The top comment concedes Zitron may still be directionally right that Anthropic and OpenAI's revenue can't cover their compute commitments, that enterprise AI mandates often outrun real utility, and that a datacenter overbuild could eventually strain insurance and private-credit markets — even while granting his specific 2024 predictions didn't hold up. That split, wrong on the calls but arguably not wrong on the risk, is why the thread ran long.

"If something is currently happening, the people saying it's impossible are probably wrong."

Gemini 3.8 Flash Ties Opus, Splits the Room

Google DeepMind · September 2, 2026 · 648 pts on HN · 385 comments

Gemini 3.8 Flash lands three to four weeks after 3.7 Flash, which itself followed 3.6 Flash on the same cadence — a monthly release rhythm that has commenters debating whether DeepMind is simply unleashed post-restructuring, or whether these were already in motion before any leadership shakeup. On Artificial Analysis's intelligence index it scores 59, matching Claude Opus 5 at medium effort (Opus 5 at max hits 61-63), and it's reportedly dominant on the DeepSWE coding benchmark, at a fraction of the cost of rival flagship models.

The model card itself became part of the story: it briefly 404'd after launch and had to be pulled from web.archive.org, and a sharp-eyed commenter caught a safety-comparison column labeled "vs Gemini 3.7 Flash" that was actually benchmarked against the older "Gemini 3 Flash" for a fifth of its rows — with multilingual safety and instruction-following both quietly regressing under that mislabeled comparison.

Skepticism centers less on the model than on Google's harness: since Gemini CLI was retired for the "Antigravity" agent in May, several developers say it requires manual approval for commits, pushes, and deploys, blunting the raw speed advantage. Google is reportedly skipping Gemini 3.5 Pro entirely and pre-training Gemini 4 already, with Flash iterating faster simply because, at a rumored 200-400B parameters, it's cheaper to retrain than Pro.

ChatGPT's Desktop App Is Secretly an Office Suite

Simon Willison · September 1, 2026 · 480 pts on HN · 235 comments

Simon Willison was clearing out his ~/.cache folder with OmniDiskSweeper when he found 1.7GB sitting in a directory called codex-primary-runtime — a full Python install, a full Node.js install, and native binaries for Poppler, git, and, improbably, the LibreOffice suite. The breakdown is oddly precise: 446.4MB of Node, 440.6MB of Python, and 429.7MB just for libreoffice-headless, all quietly bundled into what most users think of as a chat app.

The mechanism is a plugins/documents skill set that tells the Codex/ChatGPT agent how to find and invoke those binaries — meaning the app is shipping its own private office suite specifically so an AI agent can open, edit, and export documents without touching whatever's already installed on your machine. It's a pattern that says more about where agentic coding tools are headed than any single feature announcement would.

HN's reaction split between amusement and a pointed suggestion: if OpenAI is quietly depending on LibreOffice's forked-from-OpenOffice codebase to make its flagship product work, maybe it should be funding the project rather than freeriding on it. The broader thread is a quiet indictment of how much infrastructure agentic AI tools now carry around invisibly, and how little of that shows up in a changelog.

"1.7GB in the cache folder, and a full office suite nobody asked for."

44% on ARC-AGI-1, Trained in 90 Minutes

Mithil Vakde · September 2, 2026 · 633 pts on HN · 158 comments

Mithil Vakde's upgraded result: a small transformer trained from scratch in 1.5 hours on a single RTX 5090, for a total lifetime compute cost of 67 cents, scoring 44% on ARC-AGI-1's public eval — up from 40% in his earlier viral post, and roughly matching specialized architectures like TRM and HRM without their recursive machinery. It also picks up 7% on the harder ARC-2.

The gains came from unglamorous modernization: SwiGLU instead of GELU, RMSNorm over LayerNorm, NorMuon replacing AdamW, and 3D RoPE positional embeddings paired with per-task additive embeddings, which the ablations show matter more than anything else — removing either one drops scores to 25%. Oddly, switching the loss to supervised-only (dropping input-token loss) made test loss worse yet pushed eval accuracy from 40% to 44%, a result Vakde admits he can't fully explain.

The real argument, echoed approvingly by researchers like Jeremy Howard and Lucas Beyer on the earlier version, is methodological: banning offline pretraining and training strictly from init after submission is the cleanest way to guarantee no synthetic-data leakage and to make sample efficiency, not scale, the thing being measured. Vakde thinks 65% is reachable within the same transformer framework without new architecture — he's just publishing the recipe and asking others to try.

"Just a transformer with the most obvious representation. I don't understand why others didn't figure this out."

Apple's 'Shocking Evidence' Against an Ex-Engineer

9to5Mac · August 31, 2026 · 202 pts on HN · 144 comments

Apple's latest filing in its trade-secrets suit against OpenAI centers on forensic analysis of a MacBook used by Chang Liu, a former senior systems electrical engineer who left Apple for OpenAI in January. Apple alleges Liu didn't just download a confidential circuit schematic but ran it through LTspice simulations in March, and told colleagues his AI "agent" had learned to run the tool and review the output — the kind of detail that makes this an AI story as much as a trade-secrets one.

The filing also claims Liu, on learning of Apple's internal investigation, sent an OpenAI colleague instructions for destroying evidence, which she reportedly agreed to follow, and that he used an in-house tool at OpenAI sharing a name with an internal Apple engineering application. Apple says it only found the schematic-on-Mac-mini trail because that device synced via iCloud to the MacBook now under scrutiny, and it wants access to the Mac mini too.

OpenAI has called for the suit's dismissal and denies Apple has shown any confidential information was actually used. The case matters beyond its parties: Apple's own framing — that feeding trade secrets into an AI agent "may create irreversible and continually propagating uses" of that information — is a novel legal theory that other companies suing over AI-assisted IP leakage will likely borrow.

"Feeding a trade secret into a learning AI agent may create irreversible, continually propagating uses of it."

One Mac Mini, Two Models, No Subscriptions

Kevin Lewis · September 1, 2026 · 282 pts on HN · 176 comments

Kevin Lewis's setup is deliberately unglamorous: an M4 Pro Mac mini with 48GB of unified memory running two models — Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (35B total parameters, only 3B active per token as a mixture-of-experts) for anything needing depth, and a lightweight Gemma-4-E4B for routine formatting — served through oMLX and mesh-networked to his phone and MacBook via Tailscale. Setup time: about 30 minutes.

The performance numbers are concrete: 325 tokens/second on prompt processing, 34 tokens/second on generation, with the MoE architecture meaning the 35B model needs only about 20GB of RAM rather than the ~35GB a dense model that size would demand — the gap that decides whether this fits on a $600 Mac mini or requires a $3,000-plus Studio. He's since ordered a 128GB M5 Max Studio anyway.

His stated motivations track closely with why this piece was flagged for this newsletter's audience: cost predictability once the hardware is paid for, no rate limits, offline capability for background agent work, and insulation from a provider silently swapping the model behind an API. It's less a rejection of frontier cloud models than an argument for routing the 80% of requests that don't need one — Claude and GPT-tier models stay available for when they're actually required.

"Cloud APIs are rented land. They can change the model behind the scenes whenever they feel like it."

World Labs' Atlas Turns Photos Into Simulations

World Labs · September 1, 2026 · 252 pts on HN · 58 comments

Atlas, Fei-Fei Li's World Labs' new flagship, is a multimodal autoregressive diffusion transformer pretrained from scratch to handle text, images, video, and 3D inside one shared "spatial context." It generates up to a minute of camera-controlled video at 1440p, and in blind human preference tests for camera-following it beat MiniMax H3 (75% preference for Atlas), Gemini Omni Flash (81%), and Seedance 2.5 (94%).

The more consequential capability is sparse-view reconstruction: Atlas can rebuild a real scene from as few as two or three photos, reportedly outperforming specialized 3D-reconstruction models built for exactly that task, and can extend to over a hundred input images for faithful full-environment recreation — demonstrated by rebuilding Stanford's Main Quad from a couple dozen ground-level phone photos.

World Labs is explicit that the real target is robotics: turning a smartphone video of a physical space into a 3D simulation a robot can navigate and manipulate objects within, without the specialized capture rigs Real-to-Sim workflows have traditionally required. HN's small but technical thread (a cofounder showed up to answer questions) focused on the architecture choice — camera poses as a native input, versus Genie 3's raw keyboard-command approach to world interaction — as the more interesting fork in world-model design than the demo reel itself.

"The more it sees, the less it has to imagine."

Perplexity's Sources Are Mostly SEO Ghost Towns

Trellner Research · September 2, 2026 · 99 pts on HN · 49 comments

Trellner Research asked Perplexity's sonar and sonar-pro models for the best products across 380 software categories, then audited all 7,534 citations that came back. The result: 59.8% point at domains ranked worse than #100,000 on Tranco's popularity list, and 23.4% aren't in the top million at all — with a median rank of 71,611 among the sites that do rank.

Three of the most-cited domains — wifitalents.com, worldmetrics.org, and gitnux.org — share Cloudflare nameservers, an identical page template, and a combined 215,128 auto-generated "best software" pages, despite none of the three existing before December 2023. Two of them title their own homepages "Facts & Grounding Page," addressed, in the report's phrase, to the retrieval software rather than to any human reader. Separately, guideflow.com — a product-demo vendor with no stake in most categories it was cited in — turned up as the third-largest overall source, ahead of Gartner.

HN's discussion undercut the report almost as much as it engaged with it: multiple commenters flagged that the "independent research firm" itself reads as AI-generated, that its founder has no discoverable identity, and that this was the poster's fourth similar submission in an hour — a live illustration, whether intentional or not, of exactly the problem the report describes.

"The record a machine reads is not the one a company writes."
