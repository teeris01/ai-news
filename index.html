A 125B Model on a Gaming PC, at Triple-Digit Speeds

github.com/Niko1221 · 664 points · 309 comments
“Serves OpenAI and Anthropic-compatible APIs on localhost 8080 — a drop-in replacement for existing agent systems.”
Read articleHN Discussion

Strata is an open-source C++ inference engine that runs Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, on a machine with a 12–24 GB NVIDIA card and 64 GB of system RAM. The trick is a three-tier memory hierarchy: the GPU keeps the hot experts, the CPU handles cold ones with vector instructions, and an SSD holds n-gram lookup tables served through the OS page cache. Reported speeds are around 95 tokens per second on an RTX 5070 at short context and roughly 70 on an RTX 3090 at 128K, helped by multi-token prediction that accepts 2.4–3.2 tokens per pass.

The part that matters for an agent builder is the surface: Strata serves OpenAI- and Anthropic-compatible APIs on localhost:8080, so it can slot in behind existing agent harnesses without code changes. That makes it a credible offline or privacy-bound backend for tool-using workflows. The caveats are real, though: one sequential request at a time, greedy decoding only, no KV-cache reuse between conversations, slow prefill at long contexts, and little independent benchmarking so far.

With 664 points it was the week's biggest AI thread on the front page, a sign of how much appetite there is for frontier-ish capability that never leaves your desk. Treat the headline numbers as vendor-style claims until others reproduce them. HN's comment threads were not retrievable for this issue, so community reaction is reflected only in the score and the 309-comment count.

Platforms · Privacy · On-Device AI

macOS 27's Apple Intelligence Quietly Takes 20–30 GB

github.com/omlahore · 459 points · 284 comments
“There is no master switch left to stop them.”
Read articleHN Discussion

After upgrading to macOS 27, many Macs automatically download Apple's on-device models, and the footprint is far above what Apple states. Apple's own figure is up to 14 GB on M3-and-later machines; MacRumors measured about 20.7 GB on an M4 Pro Mac mini, Ars Technica 22.4 GB on an M3 MacBook Air, and some Reddit users report over 30 GB. Higher-end chips pull a larger model, reportedly Apple's AFM 3 Core Advanced.

There is no official removal path. The community workaround is deleting the generative-models folder from Recovery mode; one test dropped usage from 27.6 GB to 3.1 GB, with the caveat that the models can try to reinstall and users should back up first. The repo at the center of the HN thread packages that approach.

The story is a small case study in where on-device AI is heading: models become OS components, not apps, and the user's control over them shrinks to whatever the platform vendor allows. For anyone shipping local-model features, the lesson is that disk budget and an honest off-switch are now product requirements.

Infrastructure · AI Footprint

Improper Redaction Exposes a Google Data Center's Appetite

1011now.com · 309 points · 423 comments
“Nebraska faces persistent drought, and most state water resources support agricultural irrigation.”
Read articleHN Discussion

A redaction that wasn't properly applied surfaced water and electricity figures for a Google data center in Lincoln, Nebraska, according to local reporting. The thread drew 423 comments, more than its 309 points, which usually signals argument rather than consensus.

The backdrop is a state pushing for disclosure: Nebraska's new rules require data centers to report annual water consumption and power demand, and reporting this year put Google's Nebraska facilities at roughly 732 million gallons of water in 2025, with the company expecting that to grow. Cooling choices matter: evaporative systems drink water, closed-loop systems save water but draw more electricity.

As AI training and inference reshape load forecasts, utilities and drought-prone regions are asking for numbers companies have long treated as trade secrets. The accidental leak is a reminder that the AI buildout has a physical ledger, and that ledger is increasingly going to be public.

Will Coding Agents Finally Make Us 'Use the Platform'?

nolanlawson.com · 288 points · 299 comments
“LLMs seem to love duplicating code.”
Read articleHN Discussion

Nolan Lawson asks why developers keep reaching for npm packages when browsers now ship the feature natively. His answers are human ones: browsers spent years catching up, library sites had better docs and marketing than scattered platform references, nobody's npm search says 'just use CSS', and building things yourself is simply fun.

The most relevant section for Claude Code users is the AI angle. The optimistic case: because LLMs know the platform encyclopedically, an agent can pick exactly the right API and, after testing, favor the faster, more correct one, with no IKEA effect attached to code it wrote. The pessimistic case: LLMs love duplicating code, ignore existing helpers, and developers commit first drafts without enough testing. Lawson says he has seen both in his own use.

It is a useful frame for configuring agents: whether your harness nudges toward standard APIs and existing helpers, through instructions, tests and review, may decide which future you get. 299 comments suggests everyone has an opinion.

Networking · AI Clusters

Homa: The Case Against TCP Inside AI Clusters

youtube.com · 65 points · 29 comments
“Approximately 13x lower P99 tail latency than TCP for short messages.”
Read articleHN Discussion

John Ousterhout of Stanford argues that TCP and RDMA are structurally mismatched to modern AI traffic, which has shifted from big training transfers to many small, latency-sensitive inference messages. Two design commitments hurt: byte streams erase message boundaries and allow head-of-line blocking, and sender-driven congestion control built on single-bit ECN signals leaves senders oscillating between over- and under-sending.

Homa flips this around with message-oriented RPC semantics, receiver-driven grants, and shortest-remaining-processing-time scheduling using the switches' hardware priority queues. The reported payoff is roughly 13x lower P99 tail latency than TCP for short messages and nearly 2x better even for the largest messages tested.

The honest limit: full benefits need switch hardware support and kernel integration, so this is not a drop-in swap. Still, as agentic workloads multiply small calls between model shards and tools, tail latency becomes a first-class cost, and this is the kind of lower-layer rethink that follows.

Performance · Go

A 40 ms Go GC Pause, Caused by Swap

frn.sh · 22 points · 5 comments
“Go's garbage collector reads its metadata in a stop-the-world pause, and that metadata can be in swap.”
Read articleHN Discussion

Fernando Simões enabled swap in production to absorb memory spikes and then found stop-the-world GC pauses 800 times longer than normal. The mechanism: the kernel evicted Go's GC bookkeeping pages to swap, and when the collector read them during a pause it took 228 major page faults, 39 of the 40 ms.

The secondary cost is per-goroutine: message building went from 3–5 ms to 105 ms on NVMe and 903 ms on network storage. An update notes Go 1.26's Green Tea GC made negligible difference.

Low on points, high on practical value. The takeaway for anyone running allocation-heavy services, including agent backends and MCP servers written in Go, is that 'swap as a safety net' can quietly turn into a tail-latency problem.

github.com/net4people · 67 points · 8 comments
“Certificate verification bypass — concealed.”
Read articleHN Discussion

The post, hosted in the net4people censorship-circumvention community, alleges that Xray-core, a widely used proxy toolkit in that ecosystem, concealed a certificate verification bypass vulnerability. I could not retrieve the full write-up or comments for this issue, so details on affected versions and impact are not summarized here.

What the headline already says is enough to take seriously: certificate verification is the part of a TLS-based tunnel that tells you who you are talking to, and tools used by people evading censorship are used by people with the most to lose from a quiet downgrade.

If you run Xray-core or software built on it, check the project's advisories and the thread directly. At 67 points and 8 comments it is a small thread, but a high-consequence one.
